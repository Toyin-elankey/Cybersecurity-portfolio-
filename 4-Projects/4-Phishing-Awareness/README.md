---

## 📌 Project Overview

Attackers rarely break through a firewall — they persuade a person to open the door. This project addresses that reality by producing **end-user security awareness material** covering:

- How to **recognise phishing emails and fake websites**
- The **social-engineering tactics** attackers use to manipulate victims
- **Practical best practices** that reduce the risk of falling victim
- Reinforcement through **real-world examples and an interactive quiz**

It is the counterpart to my investigation work: the phishing incident report documents how a real campaign was analysed and contained, while this deck is the **preventive control** — reducing the number of employees who interact with the lure in the first place.

## 🗂️ Deck Contents

| Section | Slide highlights |
| ------- | ---------------- |
| **Introduction & Objectives** | Why social engineering remains the most successful attack method — it exploits trust, urgency and human error rather than technical vulnerabilities |
| **Recognising Phishing Emails** | Anatomy of a phishing email and a red-flag reference table: mismatched sender domains, manufactured urgency, generic greetings, suspicious links, unexpected attachments, requests for sensitive data, poor spelling/grammar, too-good-to-be-true offers |
| **Recognising Fake Websites** | How spoofed sites imitate legitimate ones to harvest credentials and payment data — checking the URL for misspellings and unusual TLDs, verifying HTTPS and the padlock, hovering before clicking, spotting low-resolution logos and inconsistent formatting, and typing the address directly instead of clicking a link |
| **Social Engineering Tactics** | Common tactics explained: **phishing, pretexting, baiting, vishing, smishing, tailgating/piggybacking and quid pro quo** — plus why they work (authority, urgency and fear, trust and familiarity, curiosity and reward) |
| **The Phishing Attack Life-cycle** | How an attack unfolds from reconnaissance through to exploitation, and where individuals can intervene to stop it |
| **Best Practices (Do / Don't)** | A practical two-column checklist — verify senders through a separate trusted channel, hover before clicking, report attempts, use strong unique passwords and MFA, keep systems updated, slow down on urgent requests — set against the habits that cause compromise |
| **Real-World Case Studies** | Three documented incidents showing the scale of the risk:<br>• **Google & Facebook invoice scam (2013–2015)** — forged invoices and pretexting led to over $100 million being wired to fraudulent accounts<br>• **2020 Twitter verified-account breach** — vishing of employees yielded access to internal admin tools and dozens of hijacked high-profile accounts<br>• **Target Corporation data breach (2013)** — credentials phished from a third-party HVAC vendor provided the initial foothold |
| **Interactive Assessment Quiz** | Five scenario-based questions with multiple-choice answers, covering a spoofed sender domain, vishing/pretexting for a password, indicators of a fake website, finding an unknown USB drive, and why MFA is the strongest extra protection if a password is stolen |
| **Conclusion** | Cybersecurity is not solely the responsibility of IT — every person who uses email, browses the web or answers a phone call plays a role in defence; awareness, healthy scepticism and verifying before trusting are the most reliable protections available |
